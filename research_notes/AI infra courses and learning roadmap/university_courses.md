## Key Question 1: Which courses have publicly available materials, and what is the latest offering (term/year) and URL of each?

### Takeaway
As of late 2026 the strongest open courses are all current. CMU runs four: 10-414/714 DL Systems (Fall 2026), 15-442/642 ML Systems (Spring 2026), 11-868 LLM Systems (Spring and Fall 2026) and 15-779 (Fall 2025). Stanford runs CS336 (Spring 2026), CS149 (Fall 2025) and CS217 (Winter 2026). MIT runs 6.5940 (Fall 2026, back after skipping Fall 2025) and 6.S894 (Fall 2025). UCSD runs Hao Zhang's CSE 291A/DSC 291 (Spring 2026, the successor to CSE 234 Winter 2025). The textbook mlsysbook.ai was updated in November 2026. A wave of 2025–2026 courses centred on LLM serving has also appeared: UW CSE 554 (Winter 2026), Berkeley Scalable AI (Spring 2026), UCSD CSE 291A by Yiying Zhang (Fall 2026), Michigan CSE 585 (Winter and Fall 2026), TU Berlin MLMMI (Summer Semester 2026), HSE/YSDA Efficient DL Systems (2026), Plaksha (2026) and MLC's "Modern GPU Programming for MLSys" (July 2026). Several classics are now archived: UW CSE 599W, Berkeley AI-Sys Sp22 and CS294-162 Fa24, MLC Summer 2022, Stanford CS229S (Fall 2024 is the latest) and Michigan EECS 598 W24.

### Inferences
- UCSD CSE 291A/DSC 291 (Spring 2026) is effectively the current version of Hao Zhang's CSE 234. Both have the same instructor, the same three-part structure (basics → ML systems → LLM systems) and the same scribe-notes format, and no CSE 234 Winter 2026 page exists. Learners should use the Spring 2026 site as the latest edition.
- CMU now has a full ladder of ML-systems courses with public sites: 10-414/714 (framework internals) → 15-442/642 (systems breadth) → 11-868 (LLM systems) → 15-779 (research seminar). MLC's July 2026 GPU course says it "grows out of the Machine Learning Systems course series at Carnegie Mellon", which suggests the CMU courses and the MLC materials are converging.
- Courses dedicated to LLM serving were rare before 2025. In 2025–2026 at least six appeared or were refocused: UW CSE 554, Berkeley Scalable AI, UCSD CSE 291A Fall 2026, the Georgia Tech OMSCS seminar, Plaksha and TU Berlin MLMMI. The field is now treated as its own course topic, not one lecture inside an ML-systems course.
- UW CSE 599W (no term on the page) is widely understood to be the 2018 predecessor of the CMU dlsys course. This could not be confirmed on the live page; treat it as historical only.

## Key Question 2: For each course, what are the topics, assignments, video availability, prerequisites, workload and level?

### Takeaway
The best courses for building things are CMU 10-414/714 (build the "needle" framework with a CUDA backend), CMU 11-868 (seven public assignments, from CUDA kernels to inference on TPU), Stanford CS336 (five large assignments, including Triton FlashAttention-2 and distributed training) and MIT 6.S894 (11 GPU labs up to H100 matmul and TPU). Public lecture videos are fully available only for CS336, MIT 6.5940 (prior years on YouTube, live.efficientml.ai for 2026), MLC 2022, and older editions of CMU dlsys (2022) and Stanford CS149 (2023). UCSD's recordings need a UCSD login, Berkeley's need a berkeley.edu login, and most CMU courses publish slides but no current videos.

#### Stanford CS336 Language Modeling from Scratch (Spring 2026) — https://cs336.stanford.edu/
- **Instructors:** Percy Liang and Tatsunori Hashimoto. **Recordings:** a public YouTube playlist linked from the official site. — [cs336](https://cs336.stanford.edu/)
- **Assignments:**
  - A1 Basics: tokenizer, model architecture and optimizer; train a minimal LM.
  - **A2 Systems**: "Profile and benchmark … optimize Attention with your own Triton implementation of FlashAttention2", then "Build a memory-efficient, distributed version" of the training code.
  - A3 Scaling: fit scaling laws through a training API.
  - A4 Data: filter and deduplicate Common Crawl.
  - A5 Alignment and reasoning RL: SFT and RL, with optional DPO.
  — [cs336](https://cs336.stanford.edu/)
- **Systems-relevant lectures:** resource accounting (FLOPs, memory, arithmetic intensity); GPUs and TPUs; kernels and Triton; parallelism (two lectures); **inference (lecture 10)**; guest lecture by Dan Fu. — [cs336](https://cs336.stanford.edu/)
- **Prerequisites:**
  - Python proficiency: "minimal scaffolding"; "at least an order of magnitude greater" code than other classes.
  - "strong familiarity with PyTorch and know basic systems concepts like the memory hierarchy".
  - Calculus and linear algebra, probability, and ML (CS221/229/230/124/224N).
  - It is "a 5-unit class … very implementation-heavy".
  — [cs336](https://cs336.stanford.edu/)
- **Self-study compute:** the page lists cloud prices for one B200 as of March 28, 2026 (Modal $6.25/h with $30 free each month, RunPod $4.99/h, Nebius $5.50/h, and others). It advises debugging on CPU first. — [cs336](https://cs336.stanford.edu/)
- **Community review (Andy Timm, January 10, 2026; finished December 2025):**
  - Workload averaged about 16 hours per week over 12 weeks, against Stanford's estimate of 20 hours per course week.
  - A1 took about 3 weeks, A2 about 3 weeks, A3 "1 week, if that", A4 about 2 weeks and A5 about 3 weeks.
  - Total GPU spend was $353.23.
  - The reviewer calls A2 (Triton FlashAttention plus hand-rolled DDP) the core of the systems learning, and the course "CS/SWE Hard" rather than maths-hard.

## Key Question 3: Which courses cover LLM inference and serving most thoroughly, which are best for beginners vs. advanced learners, and in what order should they be taken?

### Takeaway
CMU 11-868 is the most thorough open course on LLM inference and serving. It has about 10 serving-related lectures covering vLLM/PagedAttention, SGLang, speculative decoding, quantization, FlashAttention 1–4, DistServe, KV-cache reuse, Mooncake and TPU inference, plus public assignments.

The best open complements are:
- UCSD CSE 291A/DSC 291 (S26) for its slides and scribe notes, including a vLLM guest lecture by Woosuk Kwon.
- The HSE/YSDA inference weeks.
- Berkeley Scalable AI's lectures on vLLM, SGLang, Dynamo and TRT-LLM, whose slides and syllabus are public but whose videos are not.
- Research reading lists from Michigan CSE 585, UCSD CSE 291A (Yiying Zhang) and UW CSE 554, the last of which is a whole course on serving.

Beginners should start with mlsysbook.ai, MiniTorch or CMU dlsys (HW0 is a diagnostic), and CS229S's KV-cache and speculative-decoding project. Advanced learners should move to 15-779, MIT 6.S894, MLC "Modern GPU Programming for MLSys" and the research seminars.

### Inferences
**Proposed ordering, beginner to advanced, for an LLM-serving-focused self-learner.** This sequence is my synthesis, not a source's recommendation.

0. **Fundamentals (see Key Question 4):** Python and C++, CS:APP/15-213, architecture (CS61C or 6.004), OS (OSTEP, 6.1810 or CS162), linear algebra (18.06), and deep learning and Transformers (Karpathy's Zero to Hero, d2l and CS224n).
1. **Orientation, with a low barrier:**
   - mlsysbook.ai Volume I chapters on frameworks, training, hardware acceleration and serving, together with its slides.
   - Then MiniTorch modules 1–4 (autodiff, tensors, efficiency and GPU).
2. **Framework internals:** CMU 10-414/714, using the 2022 public videos, the current slides and needle HW0–HW4 on Colab. This gives you autodiff, NDArray, the CUDA backend and Transformers in your own framework.
3. **First inference hands-on:** CS229S Project 2 (KV cache and speculative decoding in nanoGPT) and the CS229S slides.
4. **ML systems breadth:** either of these, both of which have explicit serving lectures:
   - CMU 15-442/642 slides (GPU/CUDA, GEMM, parallelism, compilers, serving).
   - UCSD CSE 291A/DSC 291 S26 slides, scribe notes and PAs (matmul kernels, tensor-parallel communication, speculative decoding).
5. **LLM systems core:**
   - **CMU 11-868 assignments 1–7 and its serving lectures.** This is the primary track for vLLM and SGLang concepts.
   - **Stanford CS336 A1–A2** (from-scratch LM, Triton FlashAttention-2, DDP), with the inference lecture.
   - The HSE/YSDA week 8–9 inference seminars, which measure prefill and decode and implement continuous batching.
6. **Efficiency and compression:** MIT 6.5940 (quantization, pruning, the LLM deployment lab, the production-inference guest lecture). This maps directly onto the quantization and KV techniques used in vLLM and SGLang.
7. **GPU performance depth:**
   - Stanford CS149 A3/A5 (CUDA, "World's Fastest CUDA Kernels") or CMU 15-418.
   - MIT 6.S894 labs 4–6 and 10 (tiled, then tensor-core, then H100 matmul).
   - MLC "Modern GPU Programming for MLSys" (Blackwell GEMM and FlashAttention 4).
   - CMU 15-779 lectures on warp specialization and megakernels.
8. **Compilers (optional but valuable for SGLang/TVM/Triton-style work):** MLC 2022 / book.mlc.ai, then the compiler units of 15-442 and 15-779.
9. **Research frontier:** read the serving units of Michigan CSE 585, UCSD CSE 291A (Yiying Zhang), UIUC CS598 FAL, Princeton COS568, Berkeley CS294-162 and UW CSE 554. Use the Georgia Tech OMSCS outline as a checklist of serving topics: chunked prefill, P-D disaggregation, FlashInfer, multi-instance serving.
10. **Hardware perspective (optional):** Stanford CS217 and the MIT 6.5940 hardware sections.

Further judgements:
- For someone targeting vLLM or SGLang contributions, steps 2, 4, 5 and 7 give the best return. 11-868 covers vLLM and SGLang by name. 15-442 and 15-779 cover RadixAttention, which is SGLang's core idea, alongside PagedAttention. CSE 291 S26 has a guest lecture from vLLM's Woosuk Kwon.
- CS336 is not a serving course, but its Assignment 2 is arguably the best public exercise in GPU-aware thinking: Triton kernels and memory-efficient distributed code.

### Gaps
- No source compares these courses' serving coverage head-to-head. The ranking above is inferred from syllabi.
- Whether UW CSE 554 and the Georgia Tech seminar have publicly downloadable slides and assignments is unverified.
- Plaksha has posted only topics 1–5, which do not yet include the serving half.

## Key Question 4: Which CS fundamentals do these courses assume, and which free courses fill those gaps?

### Takeaway
Almost every course assumes:
- C/C++ systems programming at the level of 15-213, which CMU dlsys, 15-442 and 15-418 list as a requirement.
- Linear algebra.
- Python and PyTorch fluency.
- Basic ML and deep learning.
- For the GPU-heavy courses, computer architecture and performance engineering (6.191/6.004, and 6.106/6.172 for 6.S894).
- For the GenAI seminars, OS or distributed-systems coursework.

Free, current materials cover all of these gaps. CMU 15-213 (Fall 2026), MIT 6.004 and 6.172 on OCW, MIT 6.1810 (Fall 2026) and 6.5840 (Spring 2026), Berkeley CS61C and CS162 (Fall 2026), MIT 18.06SC, the Missing Semester, Karpathy's Zero to Hero, fast.ai, d2l.ai, and Stanford CS231n (Spring 2026) and CS224n (Winter 2026) all have live sites.

## Key Question 5: Are there expert-written guides or reading lists on how to get into ML systems, and what do they recommend?

### Takeaway
No single canonical "how to get into MLSys" guide by a professor was found. The most useful academic guidance is spread across:
- The mlsysbook.ai learning paths, which tell you to read and then build and measure (TinyTorch, MLSys·im, labs).
- Course reading lists. They consistently begin with meta-skills ("How to Read a Paper", Roscoe's reviewing guide) and then a canonical paper sequence: TensorFlow/PyTorch → TVM/Triton → ZeRO/Megatron/GPipe → FlashAttention → Orca → PagedAttention/vLLM → SGLang/RadixAttention → DistServe/Sarathi-Serve → Mooncake.
- Survey and tutorial material from the CMU/MLC group.
- A January 2026 "Survival Guide for ML Systems Research" by Michigan PhD and course GSI Jiachen Liu.

### Inferences
- Read together, the academic sources recommend the same approach:
  1. Learn to read systems papers (Keshav's "How to Read a Paper" and Roscoe's guide are near-universal first readings).
  2. Build a mini-framework (needle, MiniTorch or TinyTorch) to internalize autodiff and tensor runtimes.
  3. Learn GPU fundamentals (PMPP, GPU Glossary, GPU Puzzles) before kernels (Triton, then CUDA, then Blackwell-era TMA and warp specialization).
  4. Read the serving canon in order: Orca → PagedAttention → RadixAttention/SGLang → Sarathi-Serve → DistServe → Mooncake/NanoFlow, then the LLM inference systems surveys.
- For an engineering-focused, not research-focused, learner, Liu's guide implies that industry-scale problems (Stage 3) are learned best inside companies or open-source projects such as vLLM and SGLang. Courses take you through Stages 1–2.

- No Hacker News or Reddit threads reviewing these courses were found in the searches run. The only community review found was Andy Timm's CS336 write-up.
