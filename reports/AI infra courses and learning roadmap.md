# Open Courses Now Span Kernels to Kubernetes

A beginner can now learn AI infrastructure end to end from free, open material, but no single course does the whole job: the workable path stitches a few university courses with public materials to community lecture series, open books, engine documentation and small teaching codebases. The strongest spine as of November 2026 is Carnegie Mellon's ladder, with 10-414/714 Deep Learning Systems for building a framework and then 11-868 LLM Systems, the most thorough open course on inference and serving, with about ten serving lectures (vLLM, SGLang, prefill–decode disaggregation, KV-cache reuse) and seven public assignments; around it sit Stanford CS336 for GPU-aware building, MIT 6.5940 for efficiency, and MIT 6.S894 or MLC's July 2026 Blackwell course for kernels. The fast-moving layers are better served by "living" resources than by syllabi: GPU MODE's 114-plus recorded lectures for GPU programming, How to Scale Your Model and the Ultra-Scale Playbook for performance math and parallelism, vLLM's design docs and office hours for engine internals, and the nano-vllm, Mini-SGLang and tiny-llm teaching engines as the lab bench. Production deployment is the thinnest layer, with structured courses of 1.5 to 6 hours, so Kubernetes-scale serving is learned from project quickstarts (llm-d, NVIDIA Dynamo, KServe) and benchmarking tools. The order that course prerequisites, community roadmaps and 2026 job postings jointly support runs from fundamentals to a performance mental model, framework internals, GPU programming, the inference-engine core (ending in a self-built mini-engine), real engine internals and a first contribution, then scale-out and RL infrastructure, then production, which by my estimate takes 12–18 months at 15–20 hours a week for someone who already programs. Two hazards shape every choice: material ages in months (vLLM made Model Runner V2 its only GPU runner in v0.34.0, Hugging Face archived TGI, and 2026 Chinese roadmaps still cite vLLM files that no longer exist), and the scarcest input is not content but graded feedback and modern GPU time, because most autograders and many lecture videos are restricted to enrolled students, and Hopper- or Blackwell-specific practice needs paid GPU hours or a seat in a kernel competition.

## CMU's four-course ladder anchors a 2025–26 wave of open serving courses

University ML-systems teaching has become a public, current curriculum, and LLM serving has graduated from one lecture inside a systems course to a course topic of its own. Carnegie Mellon alone keeps live sites for four rungs: **10-414/714 Deep Learning Systems (Fall 2026, Tim Dettmers and Tianqi Chen)**, **15-442/642 Machine Learning Systems (Spring 2026, Tianqi Chen and Zhihao Jia)**, **11-868 Large Language Model Systems (Spring and Fall 2026, Lei Li)** and the research seminar 15-779, LLM Edition (Fall 2025, Jia) ([dlsyscourse.org](https://dlsyscourse.org/); [mlsyscourse.org](https://mlsyscourse.org/); [llmsystem.github.io](https://llmsystem.github.io/); [15-779](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~zhihaoj2/15-779/)). By my count at least six serving-centred courses appeared or were refocused in 2025–26, among them UW's CSE 554, whose page says flatly that "this class is about optimizing serving for large language models", and Berkeley's Scalable AI, which devotes lectures to vLLM and SGLang and to Dynamo and TensorRT-LLM; UCSD, TU Berlin, Plaksha and Georgia Tech added others ([CSE 554](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse554/26wi/); [Scalable AI](https://scalable-ai.eecs.berkeley.edu/S2026/); [UCSD CSE 291A](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~yiying/cse291a-fall26/); [TU Berlin MLMMI](https://phaniarnab.github.io/courses/mlmmi_ss2026.html); [Plaksha](https://mlsys-plaksha-26.github.io/)). Meanwhile the older canon aged out: Stanford's CS229S has no edition after Fall 2024, Berkeley's AI-Sys seminar ended in Spring 2022 and MLC's compiler course dates from Summer 2022 ([CS229S](https://cs229s.stanford.edu/fall2024/); [AI-Sys](https://ucbrise.github.io/cs294-ai-sys-sp22/); [MLC 2022](https://mlc.ai/summer22/)). The table hides a trade-off between depth and openness. **CMU 11-868 is the deepest open serving course**: its Fall 2026 syllabus covers speculative decoding, GPTQ-style quantization, FlashAttention 1 through 4, vLLM and PagedAttention, SGLang, DistServe-style disaggregation, CacheGen and CacheBlend KV reuse, Mooncake on heterogeneous hardware and two lectures on TPU inference, and its seven public assignments climb from raw CUDA through a MiniTorch-based framework and Transformer CUDA acceleration to distributed training, RLHF and JAX inference on TPU v5e ([11-868 syllabus](https://llmsystem.github.io/llmsystem2026fall/docs/Syllabus/); [11-868 homework](https://llmsystem.github.io/llmsystemhomework/)). Its prerequisites are modest, 15-122-level programming with prior ML "preferred but not required", but it mentions no lecture videos ([11-868 logistics](https://llmsystem.github.io/llmsystem2026fall/docs/Logistics/)). **Stanford CS336 strikes the opposite bargain**: every lecture is on a public YouTube playlist and Assignment 2, a Triton FlashAttention-2 plus a memory-efficient distributed trainer, is the best public exercise in GPU-aware engineering, but it has a single inference lecture and a self-described "very implementation-heavy" five-unit load ([cs336.stanford.edu](https://cs336.stanford.edu/)). One self-learner who finished it in December 2025 logged **about 16 hours a week for 12 weeks and $353.23 of GPU spend**, and found that Assignment 3 relies on a Stanford-only API and that Assignment 5's MATH dataset had been taken down ([Andy Timm review](https://andytimm.github.io/posts/cs336/cs336_review.html)). CMU 10-414/714 remains the best framework-internals course, with homeworks that grow the "needle" library into an NDArray with CPU and CUDA backends, though its autograder accounts go only to enrolled or waitlisted CMU students and its public videos are the 2022 recordings ([assignments](https://dlsyscourse.org/assignments/); [lectures](https://dlsyscourse.org/lectures/)). MIT 6.5940 calls itself "a PhD level course", but its Fall 2024 edition put lectures on YouTube with public Colab labs, and its 2026 labs end with deploying an LLM on a laptop; MIT 6.S894's eleven labs run on MIT's own Telerun cluster, so outsiders need their own NVIDIA GPU and an H100 for the tenth ([6.5940 Fall 2026](https://hanlab.mit.edu/courses/2026-fall-65940); [6.5940 Fall 2024](https://hanlab.mit.edu/courses/2024-fall-65940); [6.S894 labs](https://accelerated-computing.academy/fall25/labs/)).

Video access is the largest gap. UCSD's course recordings sit behind a campus single-sign-on page and Berkeley's behind a berkeley.edu login, CMU's 15-442 links slides but no recordings, and 11-868 publishes its syllabus and assignment handouts without mentioning videos, so a self-learner pairs CMU material with public talk series rather than waiting for a perfect course ([UCSD recordings](https://podcast.ucsd.edu/watch/wi25/cse234_a00/1); [Scalable AI](https://scalable-ai.eecs.berkeley.edu/S2026/); [15-442 schedule](https://mlsyscourse.org/schedule)). Whether UW CSE 554's slides and assignments are public could not be confirmed, because its schedule lives in an embedded spreadsheet ([CSE 554](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse554/26wi/)). The research seminars work best as curated paper lists: Michigan's CSE 585 inference unit runs from Orca and PagedAttention through DistServe and Sarathi-Serve to NanoFlow and Mooncake, and Yiying Zhang's Fall 2026 UCSD reading list adds prefix caching, disaggregation and multimodal serving ([CSE 585](https://github.com/mosharaf/cse585/tree/w26); [CSE 291A reading](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~yiying/cse291a-fall26/reading/)). Two outdated items still earn a place. CS229S's one-week Project 2, which adds KV caching and speculative decoding to Karpathy's nanoGPT, is the best first inference exercise, and Georgia Tech's paid OMSCS seminar outline, which marches from vLLM, SGLang and FlashInfer through chunked prefill, speculative decoding, disaggregation, quantization and sparse attention to multi-instance serving, doubles as a free checklist of topics ([CS229S](https://cs229s.stanford.edu/fall2024/); [Georgia Tech](https://pe.gatech.edu/courses/omscs-seminar-introduction-llm-inference-serving-systems)).

Almost every course assumes the same foundation: systems programming at CMU 15-213 level (which 15-418 calls "a strict prerequisite"), linear algebra, fluent Python and PyTorch and basic deep learning, plus architecture and performance engineering for the GPU courses, with 6.S894 describing itself as "a spiritual follow-on to 6.106" ([15-418](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~418/); [6.S894 syllabus](https://accelerated-computing.academy/fall25/syllabus/)). Free, current courses fill every gap: CMU 15-213 and Berkeley CS61C (both Fall 2026), MIT 6.1810 for operating systems (Fall 2026) and 6.5840 for distributed systems (Spring 2026), MIT 6.172 performance engineering on OpenCourseWare, the Missing Semester for tooling, and Karpathy's "Neural Networks: Zero to Hero", which builds a GPT-style Transformer from "solid programming (Python), intro-level math" ([15-213](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~213/); [CS61C](https://cs61c.org/fa26/); [6.1810](https://pdos.csail.mit.edu/6.1810/2026/); [6.5840](https://pdos.csail.mit.edu/6.824/); [6.172](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-172-performance-engineering-of-software-systems-fall-2018/); [Missing Semester](https://missing.csail.mit.edu/); [Zero to Hero](https://karpathy.ai/zero-to-hero.html)). Operating-systems memory management repays the time directly, because PagedAttention stores KV blocks behind per-sequence block tables exactly as virtual memory stores pages ([PagedAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)). The gentlest systems entry is Harvard's mlsysbook.ai, updated in November 2026, whose authors state that "no prior background in computer architecture, networking, or distributed systems is needed", and the gentlest build-it-yourself start is Cornell's MiniTorch, the same framework 11-868's assignments extend ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/); [cs249r_book](https://github.com/harvard-edge/cs249r_book); [MiniTorch](https://minitorch.github.io/)).

## GPU MODE's 114 lectures and public worklogs replace a single GPU course

GPU programming has no single best course. It is learned from a stack of free community and vendor material, nearly all of it updated during 2026, plus one paid textbook. That textbook, Hwu, Kirk and El Hajj's *Programming Massively Parallel Processors*, reached a **5th edition in 2026** that adds multi-GPU programming with NCCL and NVSHMEM and advanced matrix-multiplication chapters, though the 4th edition remains adequate and is the one CMU 11-868 assigns ([Elsevier](https://shop.elsevier.com/books/programming-massively-parallel-processors/hwu/978-0-443-43900-1); [11-868 logistics](https://llmsystem.github.io/llmsystem2026fall/docs/Logistics/)). The free reference is NVIDIA's CUDA Programming Guide, now at **v13.5 (October 31, 2026)** with new chapters on CUDA Python and tile kernels ([CUDA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-programming-guide/index.html)). For first kernels the free on-ramps are NVIDIA's one-hour "An Even Easier Introduction to CUDA" and six-hour "Getting Started with Accelerated Computing in Modern CUDA C++", the Accelerated Computing Hub's Colab notebooks, and Elliot Arledge's **12-hour freeCodeCamp CUDA course**, which runs from first kernels through matmul optimization, Triton and PyTorch extensions and has a roughly 24-hour H100 sequel ([NVIDIA training](https://www.nvidia.com/en-eu/training/online/); [NVIDIA Developer Program](https://developer.nvidia.com/join-nvidia-developer-program); [Accelerated Computing Hub](https://github.com/NVIDIA/accelerated-computing-hub); [freeCodeCamp](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/learn-cuda-programming/); [H100 sequel](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/cuda-programming-for-nvidia-h100s/)). NVIDIA's $90 CUDA Python course covers much the same ground as the Hub's free eight-hour CUDA Python syllabi, so a self-learner can skip it.

The closest thing to a living course is **GPU MODE**, a community that began as a CUDA reading group founded by Mark Saroufim and Andreas Köpf and whose recorded lecture series has passed **Lecture 114**, with code and slides in a repository of about 7,100 stars ([GPU MODE lectures](https://github.com/gpu-mode/lectures)). Its early lectures recap PMPP and teach profiling, reductions and a CUDA performance checklist; the middle ones cover Triton, CUTLASS, tensor cores, scan and SASS; and recent ones reach CuTe DSL, Gluon and multi-GPU NCCL and NVSHMEM, alongside serving-kernel talks on speculative decoding in vLLM, SGLang performance optimization and FlashInfer ([GPU MODE lectures](https://github.com/gpu-mode/lectures)). Its resource-stream repository is the best curated index of "first contact" and "second contact" CUDA material ([resource-stream](https://github.com/gpu-mode/resource-stream)).

The real kernel curriculum is a sequence of public worklogs, and its first rung has moved down a level. A performance mental model comes first, from Horace He's essay splitting runtime into compute, memory bandwidth and overhead, Modal's GPU Glossary, and the GPU chapter of How to Scale Your Model ([Making Deep Learning Go Brrrr](https://horace.io/brrr_intro.html); [GPU Glossary](https://modal.com/gpu-glossary); [scaling book GPUs](https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/gpus/)). Simon Boehm's 2022 SGEMM worklog, which climbs from 1.3% to **93.7% of cuBLAS** through coalescing, shared-memory caching, blocktiling, vectorization and warptiling, remains the classic ladder, but it is FP32 code without tensor cores on an Ampere-class card and now serves as the introduction ([Boehm](https://siboehm.com/articles/22/CUDA-MMM)). The step to current hardware runs through Aleksa Gordić's anatomy of Hopper matmul kernels, Pranjal Shankhdhar's worklog whose single-file kernel **beats cuBLAS by about 7% at N=4096 on H100** (with an NVFP4 sequel), and Colfax Research's CUTLASS tutorials, which document that Hopper's WGMMA instruction is deprecated on Blackwell in favor of tcgen05 and that consumer Blackwell cards lack Tensor Memory ([Gordić, mirror](https://yuanlehome.github.io/blog/inside-nvidia-gpus-anatomy-of-high-performance-matmul-kernels); [Shankhdhar](https://cudaforfun.substack.com/p/outperforming-cublas-on-h100-a-worklog); [Colfax](https://research.colfax-intl.com/cutlass-tutorial-writing-gemm-kernels-using-tensor-memory-for-nvidia-blackwell-gpus/)). The university complements are 6.S894's labs from tiled to tensor-core to H100 matmul, Stanford CS149's "Make the World's Fastest CUDA Kernels" assignment, and MLC's July 2026 "Modern GPU Programming for MLSys", which teaches Blackwell's tcgen05 and Tensor Memory, takes GEMM from "Tiled to SOTA" and finishes with FlashAttention 4 ([6.S894 labs](https://accelerated-computing.academy/fall25/labs/); [CS149](https://gfxcourses.stanford.edu/cs149/fall25); [MLC course](https://mlc.ai/modern-gpu-programming-for-mlsys/)). Some classics are dormant and should be read for understanding rather than current practice, notably Karpathy's llm.c (last push June 2025) and the 100-line flash-attention-minimal (last push December 2024) ([llm.c](https://github.com/karpathy/llm.c); [flash-attention-minimal](https://github.com/tspeterkim/flash-attention-minimal)).

Kernel languages consolidated around tile programming in 2025–26, and for a serving-oriented learner **Triton comes first**. It has the largest ecosystem (about 20,600 stars), an official tutorial ladder from vector addition through fused softmax and fused attention to persistent and block-scaled matmul, and Triton-Puzzles, which run on the Triton interpreter with no GPU at all ([Triton](https://github.com/triton-lang/triton); [Triton tutorials](https://triton-lang.org/main/getting-started/tutorials/index.html); [Triton-Puzzles](https://github.com/gpu-mode/Triton-Puzzles)). Enough CUDA C++ to read FlashInfer, FlashMLA and DeepGEMM comes next, then **CuTe DSL**, the Python interface of CUTLASS 4, whose tcgen05 guide claims 2–4× Hopper's WGMMA throughput; Triton's lower-level Gluon dialect, with official TMA, WGMMA and tcgen05 tutorials, is the alternative for committed Triton users ([CuTe DSL](https://docs.nvidia.com/cutlass/4.8.0/media/docs/pythonDSL/overview.html); [tcgen05 guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/cutlass/latest/media/docs/pythonDSL/guides/mma/tcgen05_programming.html); [Gluon](https://triton-lang.org/main/gluon/)). Employers confirm the direction: Perplexity's inference posting describes porting CUDA kernels to CuTe DSL for GB200 and Vera Rubin ([Perplexity](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/Perplexity/8a976851-9bef-4b07-8d36-567fa9540aef)). The newer languages are worth tracking rather than learning first. NVIDIA's cuTile launched only in December 2025, and its own quickstart and README disagree about Hopper support; PyTorch's Helion, Foundation-hosted since April 2026, compiles down to Triton; TileLang stands out for AMD portability; ThunderKittens 2.0 added full Blackwell support; and Modular's Mojo is the only one of these languages whose puzzles run on Apple-silicon GPUs ([cuTile quickstart](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cutile-python/quickstart.html); [cuTile README](https://github.com/NVIDIA/cutile-python); [Helion](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/pytorch-foundation-welcomes-helion-as-a-foundation-hosted-project-to-standardize-open-portable-and-accessible-ai-kernel-authoring); [TileLang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.17577); [TileLang on ROCm](https://rocm.blogs.amd.com/ecosystems-and-partners/rocm-tilelang-kernel/README.html); [ThunderKittens 2.0](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2026-02-19-tk-2); [Mojo GPU Puzzles](https://puzzles.modular.com/howto.html)).

Practice and feedback are nearly free; modern hardware is not. The puzzles cost nothing, LeetGPU has a free tier (a third-party comparison puts its Pro plan at $6.99 a month), and GPU MODE's KernelBot leaderboards time submissions on donated datacenter GPUs at no charge, while Tensara, also free, returned HTTP 522 errors on November 24, 2026 and should be treated as at risk ([TensorTonic](https://www.tensortonic.com/guides/leetcode-for-machine-learning); [KernelBot](https://github.com/gpu-mode/kernelbot); [Tensara](https://github.com/tensara/tensara)). Competitions now double as advanced coursework because their problems are serving hot paths: the GPU MODE–NVIDIA Blackwell hackathon set NVFP4 GEMV and GEMM problems on B200s, and AMD's GPU MODE "E2E Model Speedrun" offered a **$1.1M prize pool** for MXFP4 MoE, MLA decode and MXFP4 GEMM kernels ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/join-us-for-the-blackwell-nvfp4-kernel-hackathon-with-nvidia-and-gpu-mode/350092); [AMD](https://www.amd.com/en/developer/resources/technical-articles/2026/new-gpumode-virtual-hackathon--e2e-model-speedrun.html)). For compute, Colab's free tier (users report a T4 and 12-hour sessions) and Kaggle (about 30 GPU-hours a week by common, unofficial report) cover everything through classic kernels, and Modal's free Starter plan adds **$30 a month of credit**, which at list prices of $3.95 an hour for an H100 and $6.25 for a B200 buys about 7.6 H100-hours or 4.8 B200-hours ([gpuperhour](https://gpuperhour.com/blog/free-cloud-gpus-and-credits); [Modal pricing](https://modal.com/pricing)). Hopper and Blackwell features (TMA, WGMMA, tcgen05, NVFP4) cannot be practiced on free notebook GPUs at all, so the kernel track gets expensive exactly where it becomes job-relevant; GPU MODE's leaderboards, which have run on B200 and H100 hardware, are the main free way in ([NVIDIA on GPU MODE leaderboards](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/topping-the-gpu-mode-kernel-leaderboard-with-nvidia-cuda-compute)). AMD learners face a further caveat: Hazy Research's HipKittens work found that wave specialization, the dominant NVIDIA pattern, is the wrong design on AMD Instinct, so NVIDIA-centric tutorials do not transfer directly ([HipKittens](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-11-09-hk)).

## Serving's engine core is settled, and its frontier lives in engineering blogs

Four ideas underpin all of LLM serving, and the best explanations of them are short and free. Prefill is a compute-heavy pass over the prompt, while decode emits one token per step and is memory-bandwidth-bound at small batch; the roofline argument says how many tokens a batch needs before decode turns compute-bound, a threshold kipply derives as **208 on an A100** and the scaling book as **240 FLOPs per byte on TPU v5e** ([kipply](https://kipp.ly/transformer-inference-arithmetic/); [scaling book](https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/inference/)). KV-cache size decides concurrency: Llama-3.1-8B's grouped-query attention stores 128 KiB per token, or 16 GiB for a single 128K-token sequence, so by my arithmetic an 80 GB GPU holding its 16 GB of BF16 weights fits only about 60 concurrent 8K-token sequences, and kipply's multi-head formula would overstate that cache fourfold ([Llama-3.1-8B config](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct/raw/main/config.json)). And the metric that matters is goodput, the requests per second that meet both time-to-first-token and time-per-output-token targets, a framing from the DistServe authors ([DistServe blog](https://hao-ai-lab.github.io/blogs/distserve/)). The current starter set is kipply's 2022 napkin-math post, the scaling book's inference chapter with worked problems, Hugging Face's "Continuous batching from first principles" (November 2025), the Modular LLM Inference Handbook (BentoML's handbook, relocated after Modular acquired BentoML in February 2026) and Philip Kiely's free 256-page *Inference Engineering* from Baseten ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/blog/continuous_batching); [Modular handbook](https://handbook.modular.com/); [Modular acquisition](https://modular.com/blog/bentoml-joins-modular); [Inference Engineering](https://www.baseten.co/inference-engineering/)). Compared directly, the handbook is the best practitioner map, the scaling book the most rigorous (if TPU-centric), and Kiely's book the broadest production view, written by a vendor. The well-known 2023 overviews from NVIDIA and Lilian Weng are now history, since they predate FP4, disaggregation and wide expert parallelism ([NVIDIA 2023](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/mastering-llm-techniques-inference-optimization/); [Weng 2023](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2023-01-10-inference-optimization/)).

By late 2026 the single-GPU engine core had stopped moving, which makes it safe to learn from courses. Paged KV caches, continuous batching with chunked prefill, prefix caching, FlashAttention- and FlashInfer-class kernels, CUDA graphs with torch.compile, FP8, structured output, multi-LoRA and EAGLE-3 or native multi-token-prediction speculative decoding are standard in every major engine, and at cluster scale prefill–decode disaggregation and wide expert parallelism have become defaults; the DistServe authors' November 2025 retrospective observed that "almost every production-grade LLM serving framework … runs on disaggregation" ([Hao AI Lab](https://hao-ai-lab.github.io/blogs/distserve-retro/)). The frontier is unsettled. Attention–FFN disaggregation ranges from parity to 1.17× over wide expert parallelism in the newest measurements, and the first systematic study of speculative decoding inside a production engine found that verification dominates execution and that real speedups fall substantially short of theoretical bounds ([FastAFD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07149); [Performance or Illusion?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11580)). Even the core leaves headroom: Hazy Research measured vLLM and SGLang using **at most 50% of H100 memory bandwidth** on low-batch Llama-1B decode, because one forward pass launches around a hundred kernels ([Hazy Research](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-05-27-no-bubbles)). Model architecture has also become serving design. Per-token KV footprint fell from about 320 KiB for Llama-3.1-70B to 68.6 KiB for DeepSeek-V3's latent attention to **890 bytes for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash**, so architecture papers now belong on a serving reading list ([Llama-3.1-70B config](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Meta-Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct/raw/main/config.json); [DeepSeek-V3 config](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3/raw/main/config.json); [DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19969)). The practical rule is to learn the core from courses in dependency order (napkin math, then paged KV and batching, then kernels and quantization, then architecture-aware techniques, then multi-GPU and cluster serving) and to follow the frontier in the vLLM and LMSYS blogs, which in 2026 published the clearest material anywhere on wide expert parallelism, FP4, disaggregation and parallel drafting ([vLLM blog](https://vllm.ai/blog); [LMSYS blog](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/)).

Two engines repay deep study. **vLLM (about 97,500 stars; v0.34.0, released November 16, 2026)** is a PyTorch Foundation-hosted project, and **SGLang (about 39,000 stars; v0.5.23, with a 1.0 "stability release" under way)** sits under the non-profit LMSYS alongside an industry consortium that NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Google, Meta and others founded on November 12, 2026 ([vLLM](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm); [SGLang](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang); [PyTorch Foundation](https://pytorch.org/blog/pytorch-foundation-welcomes-vllm/); [SGLang Consortium](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-11-12-sglang-consortium)). Both spun out venture-backed companies in 2026 while staying open source, Inferact with a $150M seed at an $800M valuation and RadixArk with a $100M round led by Accel, which signals that both codebases will keep their maintainers ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/01/22/inference-startup-inferact-lands-150m-to-commercialize-vllm/); [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/05/05/nvidia-amd-back-100m-round-ai-tooling-startup-radixark/)). Their feature sets are converging, so learning one makes the other legible. The rest of the field matters less for learners: TensorRT-LLM removed its TensorRT backend in version 1.2 and is now PyTorch-native, llama.cpp reached v1.0.0 with semantic versioning, and Hugging Face archived TGI on March 21, 2026, which retires every TGI-based tutorial ([TensorRT-LLM](https://nvidia.github.io/TensorRT-LLM/latest/release-notes.html); [llama.cpp v1](https://huggingface.co/blog/ggml-org/llama-cpp-v1); [TGI](https://github.com/huggingface/text-generation-inference)).

vLLM has the richer official learning stack. Its documentation includes **32 design documents** on the process architecture, PagedAttention, automatic prefix caching, the hybrid KV-cache manager, CUDA graphs, torch.compile and Model Runner V2, together the closest thing to an internals textbook, and its biweekly office hours have reached **59 recorded sessions**, of which #22 (an introduction to V1) and #35 ("How to Build and Contribute to vLLM") matter most to newcomers ([vLLM architecture overview](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/design/arch_overview/); [vLLM docs index](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/search/search_index.json); [office hours](https://vllm.ai/office-hours)). It also has the only official structured course, DeepLearning.AI and Red Hat's **"Fast & Efficient LLM Inference with vLLM"** (June 2026; 1 hour 38 minutes, nine video lessons and three code labs), which walks the quantize, serve and benchmark loop with LLM Compressor and GuideLLM; enrollment is free, though the graded quiz and certificate require the paid tier, and it teaches usage rather than internals ([DeepLearning.AI](https://www.deeplearning.ai/short-courses/fast-and-efficient-llm-inference-with-vllm/)). SGLang's documentation, now at docs.sglang.io, has strong feature-design pages (HiCache, disaggregation, expert parallelism) and 177 model cookbooks but no single architecture overview, and its sgl-learning-materials repository stopped adding videos in January 2025 and still links to the old docs site ([SGLang docs index](https://docs.sglang.io/llms.txt); [sgl-learning-materials](https://github.com/sgl-project/sgl-learning-materials)). For SGLang, community walkthroughs and the official teaching engine therefore carry more weight.

The best community deep dives are Aleksa Gordić's "Inside vLLM" (September 2025, republished on the official blog), the most thorough tour of the V1 engine; Infomaniak's April 2026 code-level trace of one chat request through 14 hops of vLLM v0.18.0; and Chenyang Zhao's bilingual Awesome-ML-SYS-Tutorial, whose SGLang walkthroughs, from the life of a request to "When SGLang OOMs, What Exactly Runs Out of Memory?", are the deepest public notes on SGLang internals ([Inside vLLM](https://vllm.ai/blog/2025-09-05-anatomy-of-vllm); [Infomaniak](https://engineering.infomaniak.com/vllm-ultimate-guide-part-1/); [Awesome-ML-SYS-Tutorial](https://github.com/zhaochenyang20/Awesome-ML-SYS-Tutorial)). Each must be read against its version. "Inside vLLM" analyzes an August 2025 commit, and in v0.34.0 "Model Runner V1 is gone", replaced by a rewrite whose largest file is under 1,300 lines against the old runner's 6,700-plus, while the default KV block size rose from 16 to 64 tokens ([v0.34.0 release](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/releases/tag/v0.34.0); [Model Runner V2](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-03-24-mrv2)). The scheduler and KV-cache concepts in older walkthroughs still hold; their model-runner sections do not.

Small teaching engines are where serving is actually learned, and four stand out from a crowded field of forks:

| Engine | Size | What it implements | Runs on | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [nano-vllm](https://github.com/GeeeekExplorer/nano-vllm) | About 1,200 lines of Python | Prefix caching, tensor parallelism, torch.compile and CUDA graphs behind a vLLM-style `LLM.generate`; offline only; 1,434 tok/s versus vLLM's 1,362 on Qwen3-0.6B | One NVIDIA GPU (benchmarked on an 8 GB laptop RTX 4070) | Reading a whole vLLM-style engine in a weekend |
| [Mini-SGLang](https://github.com/sgl-project/mini-sglang) | About 5,000 lines, versus nearly 300,000 in SGLang | Radix cache, chunked prefill, overlap scheduling, tensor parallelism, FlashAttention-3 and FlashInfer kernels, an OpenAI-compatible server | Linux with a CUDA GPU | The closest small replica of a production online engine |
| [tiny-llm](https://github.com/skyzh/tiny-llm) | Small enough to read end to end; a four-week course with tests | Qwen3 from MLX primitives, then KV cache and quantized kernels, then a "Mini vLLM" with continuous batching, chunked prefill and paged attention | Apple-silicon Mac | The most pedagogical, test-driven path |
| [gpt-fast](https://github.com/meta-pytorch/gpt-fast) | Under 1,000 lines | Batch-1 latency with int8/int4 quantization, speculative decoding and tensor parallelism | NVIDIA or AMD GPU | A torch.compile reference, not a serving engine; quiet since August 2025 |

The table's sizes and benchmarks are the projects' own claims ([nano-vllm README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GeeeekExplorer/nano-vllm/HEAD/README.md); [Mini-SGLang](https://lmsys.org/blog/2025-12-17-minisgl/); [gpt-fast README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/meta-pytorch/gpt-fast/HEAD/README.md)). tiny-llm describes itself as "an LLM-serving counterpart to CMU's Needle project", which makes it the natural sequel to 10-414/714, and the SGLang team now uses Mini-SGLang as a teaching vehicle, running a summit workshop in which attendees repaired its optimizations with a local coding agent ([tiny-llm README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/skyzh/tiny-llm/HEAD/README.md); [SGLang Summit recap](https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-11-17-sglang-summit-recap/)).

The path ends in contribution, and both projects make the first step cheap. vLLM's contributor guide supports a Python-only development install through `VLLM_USE_PRECOMPILED=1`, requires DCO sign-off and keeps a job board of good first issues and onboarding tasks, while SGLang's guide mirrors unit tests to `python/sglang/srt/` modules and calls documentation "a good way for new contributors to learn the SGLang codebase" ([vLLM contributing](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/contributing/); [SGLang contribution guide](https://docs.sglang.io/docs/developer_guide/contribution_guide.md)). On November 24, 2026, vLLM had 14 open good-first-issues and SGLang 46, and vLLM's v0.34.0 release alone drew **120 first-time contributors among 412** ([vLLM issues](https://img.shields.io/github/issues/vllm-project/vllm/good%20first%20issue.json); [SGLang issues](https://img.shields.io/github/issues/sgl-project/sglang/good%20first%20issue.json); [v0.34.0](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/releases/tag/v0.34.0)). The bar is shifting: at the November 2026 SGLang Summit the team said "agents now produce most merged PRs", so a human newcomer stands out through careful scoping, reproducible benchmarks and accuracy checks rather than volume ([SGLang Summit recap](https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-11-17-sglang-summit-recap/)). Beyond any course, xlite-dev's Awesome-LLM-Inference (updated November 2026) and AmberLJC's LLMSys-PaperList (October 2026) are the maintained literature indexes, while several popular lists, such as horseee's Awesome-Efficient-LLM, went quiet in mid-2025 ([Awesome-LLM-Inference](https://github.com/xlite-dev/Awesome-LLM-Inference); [LLMSys-PaperList](https://github.com/AmberLJC/LLMSys-PaperList); [Awesome-Efficient-LLM](https://github.com/horseee/Awesome-Efficient-LLM)).

## Two web books and torchtitan carry distributed training, and RL welds it to serving

Distributed training has the best free long-form material of any layer, built around two complementary 2025 web books. Hugging Face's **Ultra-Scale Playbook** teaches what data, tensor, context, pipeline and expert parallelism each do and how to implement them on GPUs, drawing on 4,000 scaling experiments on up to 512 GPUs, while Google DeepMind's **How to Scale Your Model** teaches how to decide quantitatively whether a given sharding is compute-, memory- or communication-bound, with collective cost models and worked problems ([Ultra-Scale Playbook](https://nanotron-ultrascale-playbook.static.hf.space/index.html); [How to Scale Your Model](https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/)). The official PyTorch tutorials on DDP, FSDP2 (which states that "FSDP1 is deprecated"), tensor parallelism and pipelining were all revised in 2025 and map directly onto torchtitan's building blocks ([FSDP2 tutorial](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/intermediate/FSDP_tutorial.html)). The hands-on companion is **picotron**, whose data-, tensor-, pipeline- and context-parallel files each run under 300 lines yet reach 38% MFU on LLaMA-2-7B across 64 H100s, paired with a picotron_tutorial video series that rebuilds it step by step ([picotron](https://github.com/huggingface/picotron); [picotron_tutorial](https://github.com/huggingface/picotron_tutorial)). Stas Bekman's actively maintained *Machine Learning Engineering Open Book* covers what courses skip (network fabrics, storage, SLURM, debugging hangs and fault tolerance), and Hugging Face's Smol Training Playbook shows the infrastructure of a real run, 384 H100s for roughly a month ([ML Engineering Open Book](https://github.com/stas00/ml-engineering); [Smol Training Playbook](https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingFaceTB/smol-training-playbook)). EleutherAI's Transformer Math 101 (2023) remains the back-of-envelope reference for compute and memory, and Sasha Rush's LLM Training Puzzles, unchanged since January 2024, still make good exercises ([Transformer Math 101](https://blog.eleuther.ai/transformer-math/); [LLM Training Puzzles](https://github.com/srush/LLM-Training-Puzzles)).

Every major framework is active, but NVIDIA's has been restructured and two popular starting points have become traps. **torchtitan** (v0.4.0, October 2026), "a clean-room, PyTorch-native implementation of large-scale training" that composes FSDP, tensor, pipeline, context and expert parallelism and supports MXFP8 and NVFP4 training, is the most readable production-grade codebase; Megatron Core (v0.20), whose README pitches Megatron-LM for "learning distributed training", is the performance and MoE reference; DeepSpeed remains the reference for ZeRO and offloading; and MaxText pairs with the scaling book for JAX on TPUs ([torchtitan](https://github.com/pytorch/torchtitan); [Megatron-LM](https://github.com/NVIDIA/Megatron-LM); [DeepSpeed](https://github.com/deepspeedai/DeepSpeed); [MaxText](https://github.com/AI-Hypercomputer/maxtext)). The traps are NeMo 2.0 LLM tutorials, since NVIDIA's NeMo repository pivoted to speech and multimodal models in 2026 and its LLM successor, Megatron-Bridge, freezes on November 30, 2026 to merge into Megatron-LM, and torchtune, whose README says it "is no longer actively maintained" ([NeMo](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/Speech); [unified Megatron plan](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/megatron-bridge/nightly/unified-megatron.html); [torchtune](https://github.com/meta-pytorch/torchtune)). Two published designs make the best case studies. DeepSeek-V3 trained on 2,048 H800s without tensor parallelism, using 16-way pipeline and 64-way expert parallelism with ZeRO-1 and FP8 for 2.788M GPU-hours, while Llama 3 used 4D parallelism on up to 16K H100s and recorded **466 job interruptions in 54 days, about 78% of the unexpected ones hardware-related**, yet still achieved over 90% effective training time ([DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437); [Llama 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783)).

RL post-training is where training and serving meet, and it now has a current map. Hugging Face's March 2026 survey "Keep the Tokens Flowing" compared 16 open-source RL libraries and found that "every library in this survey has independently converged on the same architectural principle": rollouts run on an inference engine (vLLM or SGLang) separate from the trainer, NCCL broadcast is the default way to push new weights, and Ray orchestrates 8 of the 16 ([HF survey](https://huggingface.co/blog/async-rl-training-landscape)). It supersedes Anyscale's useful but partly outdated 2025 comparison, which marked verl's asynchronous support as missing or in progress ([Anyscale](https://www.anyscale.com/blog/open-source-rl-libraries-for-llms)). The sensible learning order is the RL loop first, in TRL or in Prime Intellect's verifiers with prime-rl; then **verl** (about 24,300 stars), the open-source HybridFlow, whose colocated 3D-HybridEngine makes the resharding between training and generation layouts explicit; then **slime**, which connects Megatron with SGLang, and its enterprise sibling Miles for anyone headed toward SGLang; and finally AReaL or PipelineRL for fully asynchronous designs ([TRL](https://github.com/huggingface/trl); [prime-rl](https://github.com/PrimeIntellect-ai/prime-rl); [verl](https://github.com/verl-project/verl); [slime](https://github.com/THUDM/slime); [Miles](https://github.com/radixark/miles); [AReaL](https://github.com/areal-project/AReaL); [PipelineRL](https://github.com/ServiceNow/PipelineRL)). What this teaches a serving engineer is concrete: pushing weights into a live engine, sleeping and waking engines in colocated mode, partial rollouts, and train–inference numerical mismatch, which Thinking Machines traced to batch-dependent kernels and which now has deterministic or batch-invariant modes in SGLang, vLLM and torchtitan's TitanRL ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/); [SGLang](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2025-09-22-sglang-deterministic/); [vLLM](https://vllm.ai/blog/2025-11-10-bitwise-consistent-train-inference); [torchtitan](https://github.com/pytorch/torchtitan)). The Awesome-ML-SYS-Tutorial maintainer adds a warning worth heeding: "A large number of RL conclusions derived from papers are based on RL infrastructure in the open-source community that may be extremely flawed" ([Awesome-ML-SYS-Tutorial](https://github.com/zhaochenyang20/Awesome-ML-SYS-Tutorial)). The structured entry points are CS336's Assignment 5 (SFT and RL for reasoning) and 11-868's introductory RLHF assignment ([cs336](https://cs336.stanford.edu/); [11-868 homework](https://llmsystem.github.io/llmsystemhomework/)).

## Kubernetes serving reached 1.0 in 2026, but its courses top out at six hours

The layer around the engine settled on Kubernetes during 2026 and reached 1.0 almost everywhere, which finally makes it learnable. NVIDIA's Dynamo went GA at 1.0 in March, KServe declared its LLMInferenceService production-ready in v0.17 the same month, llm-d entered the CNCF Sandbox on March 24 and shipped 1.0 on October 20, LeaderWorkerSet (with DisaggregatedSet for prefill and decode roles) reached v1.0 in October, and Kubernetes' WG Serving disbanded in February after handing its work to permanent SIGs ([Dynamo 1.0](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/dynamo-1-0); [KServe 0.17](https://kserve.github.io/website/blog/kserve-0.17-release); [llm-d joins CNCF](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2026/03/24/welcome-llm-d-to-the-cncf-evolving-kubernetes-into-sota-ai-infrastructure/); [llm-d blog](https://llm-d.ai/blog); [LeaderWorkerSet](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/lws); [WG Serving](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2026/02/26/kubernetes-wg-serving-concludes-following-successful-advancement-of-ai-inference-support/)). For a learner the projects differ mainly in philosophy. vLLM's production-stack Helm chart is the lightest reference, with tutorials that start from minikube; llm-d bets on upstream Kubernetes APIs and packages "well-lit paths" such as an optimized baseline, a tiered prefix cache and wide expert parallelism; Dynamo is vertically integrated with its own operator, KV-aware router and planner; KServe is the CNCF control plane, now built on llm-d; and Ray Serve LLM is the Python-first option ([production-stack](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/vllm-project/production-stack@main/README.md); [llm-d README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/llm-d/llm-d@main/README.md); [Dynamo architecture](https://docs.nvidia.com/dynamo/latest/design-docs/overall-architecture); [Ray Serve LLM](https://docs.ray.io/en/latest/serve/llm/index.html)). Because all of them converge on vLLM or SGLang engines, NIXL for KV transfer and the Gateway API Inference Extension for routing, concepts learned in one transfer to the rest. No neutral head-to-head benchmark of these frameworks exists; InferenceX's Dynamo and llm-d configurations are the closest public comparison ([InferenceX](https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/about)).

Structured courses at this layer are short, and **no free multi-week MOOC on Kubernetes-scale LLM serving exists**. Beyond DeepLearning.AI's vLLM course, the useful ones are DeepLearning.AI and Predibase's "Efficiently Serving LLMs" (2 hours 9 minutes, building KV caching, continuous batching, quantization and multi-LoRA serving from first principles, though its LoRAX server last released in January 2025), the Hugging Face-taught "Quantization in Depth", Anyscale Academy's free "LLM Serving Foundations" (24 lessons and about two hours of video on Ray Serve LLM), and NVIDIA DLI's free two-hour NIM introduction ([Efficiently Serving LLMs](https://www.deeplearning.ai/short-courses/efficiently-serving-llms/); [LoRAX](https://github.com/predibase/lorax); [Quantization in Depth](https://www.deeplearning.ai/short-courses/quantization-in-depth/); [Anyscale Academy](https://courses.anyscale.com/courses/llm-serving-foundations); [NVIDIA DLI](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/training/self-paced-courses/)). KodeKloud's six-hour "AI Infrastructure Explained (GPUs, vLLM, and LLM-D)" on Coursera is the gentlest llm-d on-ramp but sits behind Coursera Plus ([Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/learn/ai-infrastructure-gpus-vllm-llmd)). Classic MLOps courses do not fill the gap: MLOps Zoomcamp has no live cohort in 2026 and teaches pre-LLM deployment, LLM Zoomcamp 2026 teaches application operations (RAG, evaluation and monitoring) rather than self-hosted serving, Full Stack Deep Learning warns that its own 2023 bootcamp is dated, and the Hugging Face LLM course's deployment chapter still centers TGI ([MLOps Zoomcamp](https://github.com/DataTalksClub/mlops-zoomcamp); [LLM Zoomcamp](https://github.com/DataTalksClub/llm-zoomcamp); [FSDL](https://www.fullstackdeeplearning.com/llm-bootcamp); [HF LLM course](https://huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/en/chapter2/8)). The depth lives in documentation and books instead: Kiely's free *Inference Engineering*, whose production chapter covers containers, autoscaling and multi-cloud capacity, and three paid O'Reilly titles, Huß and Zonca's *Generative AI on Kubernetes* (February 2026), Chip Huyen's *AI Engineering* (chapter 9 on inference optimization) and Chris Fregly's roughly 1,060-page *AI Systems Performance Engineering* ([Inference Engineering](https://www.baseten.co/inference-engineering/book/); [Generative AI on Kubernetes](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/generative-ai-on/9781098171919/); [AI Engineering](https://oreilly.com/library/view/ai-engineering/9781098166298/ch09.html); [AI Systems Performance Engineering](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/ai-systems-performance/9798341627772/)).

Benchmarking and profiling are what courses teach least and inference job postings ask for most consistently, from Anthropic's call for roofline analysis to Baseten's custom performance suites for long context and KV-cache reuse ([Anthropic](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/anthropic/jobs/5224564008); [Baseten](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/baseten/75d7beac-0298-40fa-b206-2e0c0c08a64f)). The methodology has traps: NVIDIA notes that GenAI-Perf excludes time-to-first-token from its average while LLMPerf includes it, so numbers from different tools mislead unless definitions are checked ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/llm-benchmarking-fundamental-concepts)). The maintained tools are `vllm bench sweep`, which plots per-user against per-GPU throughput and whose docs recommend sweeping concurrency rather than request rate; GuideLLM, whose v0.8 searches for the highest load that still meets an SLO; NVIDIA's AIPerf, which replaced GenAI-Perf; and Kubernetes SIG Scalability's inference-perf. Ray's LLMPerf was archived in December 2025 ([vLLM sweeps](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/benchmarking/sweeps/); [GuideLLM](https://github.com/vllm-project/guidellm); [GuideLLM v0.8](https://discuss.pytorch.kr/t/guidellm-v0-8-0-slo-feat-vllm/12149); [AIPerf](https://docs.nvidia.com/aiperf/getting-started/migrating-from-gen-ai-perf); [inference-perf](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/inference-perf); [LLMPerf](https://github.com/ray-project/llmperf)). For reference points, MLPerf Inference v6.1 (September 2026) drew 30 organizations and 486 results, and SemiAnalysis's open-source InferenceX benchmarks engines continuously across NVIDIA, AMD and Google hardware, including an agentic long-context workload called AgentX ([MLPerf v6.1](https://www.hpcwire.com/off-the-wire/mlcommons-sets-participation-record-with-new-mlperf-inference-v6-1-benchmark-results/); [InferenceX](https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/about)). Capacity planning is best learned from NVIDIA's September 2026 sizing guide, which makes FP8 the first quantization step (cutting Llama-3.1-8B's weights from 16.06 GB to 9.08 GB), and from GKE's finding that autoscaling on GPU utilization overprovisions, with queue size recommended instead ([NVIDIA sizing](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/how-to-size-gpus-for-ai-inference-and-tco-without-overspending); [GKE autoscaling](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kubernetes-engine/docs/best-practices/machine-learning/inference/autoscaling)). For local deployment, practitioner guides agree on Ollama to start, llama.cpp for control and edge devices, and vLLM once several users share a GPU ([Red Hat](https://developers.redhat.com/articles/2026/06/15/llamacpp-vs-vllm-choosing-right-local-llm-inference-engine); [dev.to comparison](https://dev.to/thurmon_demich/ollama-vs-llamacpp-vs-vllm-which-should-you-use-in-2026-10gp)).

## An eight-stage path leads from first principles to a first merged PR

The order below synthesizes the courses' stated prerequisites, the community roadmaps and 2026 job postings. The roadmaps agree more than they differ. roadmap.sh's 2026 "Inference Engineering" roadmap and Kiely's book both run from metrics and model mechanics through bottleneck analysis, GPU architecture, engines, benchmarking, optimization techniques and parallelism to production, though the roadmap mirrors the book's chapters so closely that the two count as one vote; Chinese role roadmaps published in 2026 likewise put fundamentals first, vLLM internals second and a mini-engine capstone last ([roadmap.sh](https://roadmap.sh/inference-engineering); [Inference Engineering contents](https://www.baseten.co/inference-engineering/llms.txt); [Chinese inference-framework roadmap](https://gitcode.csdn.net/6a100a9710ee7a33f274631f.html)). Their main disagreement is where CUDA belongs, ranging from first and deep in kernel-oriented plans to absent from platform-oriented ones, and job postings resolve it. An Anthropic inference performance role says that reasoning about memory bandwidth, kernel overheads and collectives "matters more than having written kernels yourself", while Inferact's and Perplexity's engine roles want CUDA, Triton and CuTe ([Anthropic](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/anthropic/jobs/5224564008); [Inferact co-op](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/inferact/2b6032f9-12a5-4083-b5e6-4bec5376cbaa); [Perplexity](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/Perplexity/8a976851-9bef-4b07-8d36-567fa9540aef)). So everyone should learn GPU programming to the point of writing attention in Triton, and only the kernel track continues into Hopper and Blackwell.

The time column is calibrated against two data points: the CS336 reviewer's 16 hours a week for 12 weeks, and a Chinese inference-framework plan that budgets about 520 hours over six months for the CUDA-to-mini-engine portion alone ([Andy Timm review](https://andytimm.github.io/posts/cs336/cs336_review.html); [Chinese inference-framework roadmap](https://gitcode.csdn.net/6a100a9710ee7a33f274631f.html)). Summed, stages 1 through 7 take roughly 12–18 months at 15–20 hours a week for someone who already programs, and stage 0 adds whatever is missing. Money matters less than it seems until stage 4, because everything earlier runs on free notebooks and Modal's monthly credit covers short single-GPU experiments, but CS336-scale work cost one learner about $350 and Hopper- or Blackwell-specific practice needs paid hours.

Three sequencing choices deserve defense. Running vLLM in stage 1, through the DeepLearning.AI course, gives every later abstraction a concrete referent before the learner reads any engine source. Building a mini-engine in stage 4 before reading vLLM in stage 5 matters because vLLM's old GPU model runner alone exceeded 6,700 lines, while nano-vllm's entire engine is about 1,200 ([Model Runner V2](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-03-24-mrv2); [nano-vllm](https://github.com/GeeeekExplorer/nano-vllm)). And scale-out follows single-GPU mastery because tensor and expert parallelism, collectives and disaggregation only make sense once a learner can explain one GPU's behavior; learners aiming at platform roles can swap stages 6 and 7. After stage 5 the path forks along the lines the postings draw: an engine-and-kernel branch that adds PyTorch internals, CuTe DSL or Gluon, MLC's Blackwell course and GPU MODE competitions; a serving-platform branch that adds Go, Rust or C++, Kubernetes operators, routing, autoscaling, NCCL and RDMA, and distributed KV stores such as Mooncake and LMCache; and an RL-infra branch built on verl and slime. Modal's inference research posting states the shared target, asking for fluency "in the LLM serving stack, from kernels and quantization up to schedulers and autoscaling", and Inferact lists as bonuses having "implemented core features in vLLM" and having "written widely-shared technical blogs or side projects on vLLM or LLM inference", which argues for publishing every stage's milestone ([Modal](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/modal/73c97bbc-8e27-4c5d-b38b-90b3afdb0d93); [Inferact](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/inferact/43c0ca54-fcf5-41fa-83a1-38800c75ccc0)).

Readers of Chinese have a parallel route with one blind spot. ZOMI's AIInfra course walks LLM-era clusters, communication, cloud-native infrastructure, distributed training and distributed inference with notebooks, including coded lessons on KV caching, PagedAttention and continuous batching, though it has slowed since December 2025; openmlsys's second edition, rebuilt during 2026 but still marked "launching soon", adds GPU/CUDA/Triton, model-serving, RL-systems and GPU-cluster chapters; Datawhale's self-llm (about 32,800 stars) is the most active hands-on deployment cookbook and uniquely covers Ascend, AMD, Moore Threads and MetaX hardware; LeetCUDA offers more than 200 graded CUDA, Triton and CuTe kernels with a PDF updated November 20, 2026; and UCSD's CSE 291A/DSC 291 site even has a Chinese toggle ([AIInfra](https://github.com/Infrasys-AI/AIInfra); [openmlsys](https://github.com/openmlsys/openmlsys); [self-llm](https://github.com/datawhalechina/self-llm); [LeetCUDA](https://github.com/xlite-dev/LeetCUDA); [CSE 291A/DSC 291](https://haoailab.com/cse291-s26/)). None of these teaches vLLM or SGLang internals in depth, and two 2026 Chinese roadmaps send learners to files such as `vllm/core/scheduler.py` that return 404 on vLLM's main branch, where the scheduler now lives under `vllm/v1/core/sched/` ([Chinese roadmap](https://gitcode.csdn.net/6a100a9710ee7a33f274631f.html); [vLLM V1 scheduler](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/main/vllm/v1/core/sched/scheduler.py)).

## Conclusion

The open AI-infra curriculum has crossed a threshold: by late 2026 nothing on this path lacks a free, current and good explanation, so the binding constraints have become feedback and hardware rather than content. The courses that teach serving most deeply withhold videos or autograders from outsiders, and the kernel skills employers now name cannot run on free notebook GPUs, which turns GPU MODE leaderboards, kernel competitions and open-source code review into the practical substitutes for graders and makes a merged vLLM or SGLang pull request the nearest thing the field has to a final exam. Academic advice points the same way: a January 2026 ML-systems research guide tells students to leave "Industry-Scale Deployment" to internships and full-time roles, and open-source engines are the one place a self-learner can practice that stage without either ([ML systems survival guide](https://amberljc.github.io/blog/2026-01-03-mlsys-survival.html)). Knowledge has also split by speed. Syllabi now teach what production adopted about a year earlier, as when 11-868's Fall 2026 edition covers DistServe and Mooncake after the DistServe authors had already called disaggregation near-universal in late 2025, while 2026's open questions, from attention–FFN disaggregation to FP4 attention and parallel drafting, live only in engineering blogs and on arXiv. The durable skill is therefore not any syllabus but the habit of reading primary sources pinned to a version and checking them with a benchmark.

The layering this report uses is itself dissolving. RL post-training puts inference engines inside training loops, and model architecture now sets serving cost, with per-token KV shrinking roughly 370-fold from Llama-3.1-70B to DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash through combined architecture and precision changes; as coding agents absorb routine patches, human value moves toward scoping, measurement and review. The beginner who will be most employable in two years treats deployment, kernels, training and model architecture as one system and can back every claim about it with reproducible numbers, which is exactly what the stage milestones above are built to produce.
